# Discussion

**30-minute accessibility | 56 SA1s | Six service categories | Private car vs public bus**

The analysis demonstrates large quantifiable differences in accessibility to essential services between private cars and public buses across Burnie, Tasmania. Private cars generally provide greater accessibility, although the magnitude of this advantage varies spatially.

These findings reflect the importance of transport networks and service distribution in shaping access to everyday opportunities (Geurs & van Wee, 2004; Levinson & Wu, 2020).

## 1. Spatial Patterns of Car Dependency

The 30-minute accessibility maps reveal a widespread advantage for private cars across Burnie's 56 Statistical Areas Level 1 (SA1s). Higher Car Dependency Index (CDI) values are common throughout the study area, while parts of northern urban Burnie demonstrate smaller differences between car and public bus accessibility.

The patterns displayed in CDI and accesibility analysis are explained by the concentration of essential services in northern Burnie. However, the accessibility differences cannot be explained by service location alone, as transport-network connectivity and public transport availability also influence access to destinations (Wu et al., 2021).

### High Car Dependency Does Not Always Mean Good Accessibility

An important finding is that **high car dependency does not necessarily indicate high overall accessibility**.

For example, selected SA1s demonstrate different private-car accessibility scores despite having the same CDI value:

| SA1 code | CDI | Car accessibility | Bus accessibility |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 60401107805 | 1.000 | 1.00 | 0.00 |
| 60401107601 | 1.000 | 0.77 | 0.00 |
| 60401107604 | 1.000 | 0.01 | 0.00 |

*Values are rounded as displayed in the interactive map.*

Although these SA1s have a CDI of 1.000, their private-car accessibility scores differ considerably. In particular, SA1 60401107604 has very limited accessibility even by private car.

This distinction is important because CDI measures the **relative accessibility advantage of one transport mode over another**, rather than the overall availability of accessible opportunities.

Therefore, CDI should be interpreted alongside both car and public transport accessibility scores. This interpretation is consistent with accessibility research emphasising the importance of considering reachable opportunities rather than transport characteristics alone (Geurs & van Wee, 2004; Levinson & Wu, 2020).

## 2. Car Dependency and Socio-Economic Disadvantage

The relationship between car dependency and socio-economic disadvantage was examined using the Australian Bureau of Statistics (ABS) Index of Relative Socio-economic Disadvantage (IRSD).

The analysis included **54 SA1s** with available CDI and IRSD values.

The scatter plot presented in the Results section shows a positive association between CDI and IRSD:

- **Pearson correlation:** r = 0.333
- **Spearman rank correlation:** ρ = 0.489
- **Sample size:** n = 54 SA1s

These results indicate that higher CDI values tended to occur alongside higher IRSD scores in the analysed SA1s.

According to the ABS (2023), lower IRSD scores indicate relatively greater socio-economic disadvantage, while higher scores indicate relatively less disadvantage.

Therefore, the positive association suggests that greater car dependency was not consistently concentrated in the most socio-economically disadvantaged areas of Burnie.

However, the relationship is not uniform, and individual SA1s may still experience both high car dependency and greater socio-economic disadvantage.

Research on car dependency highlights the importance of considering urban structure and transport alternatives when interpreting spatial differences in reliance on private vehicles (Wiersma et al., 2021; Campanelli et al., 2026).

### Implications for Transport Equity

The findings demonstrate why car dependency and socio-economic disadvantage should be examined together rather than independently.

Areas with high CDI and low IRSD may warrant further investigation because residents with limited access to private vehicles could experience additional barriers to reaching essential services.

However, the analysis does not directly measure household car ownership, transport affordability or individual travel behaviour.

Consequently, the observed relationships describe **area-level accessibility patterns** and should not be interpreted as evidence of individual residents' transport circumstances.

## 4. What This Means for Burnie

The results highlight several accessibility patterns that may be relevant to transport planning.

| Accessibility pattern | Potential planning implication |
| --- | --- |
| **High car accessibility but limited bus accessibility** | Investigate opportunities to improve public transport connections to essential services |
| **Limited accessibility by both transport modes** | Examine service locations, network connectivity and broader accessibility constraints |
| **High CDI combined with low IRSD** | Investigate potential transport-equity barriers affecting socio-economically disadvantaged communities |
| **Smaller differences between car and bus accessibility** | Examine existing public transport connections and their potential relevance to other areas |

Improving accessibility does not necessarily require increasing private-car mobility. Accessibility-focused planning can also consider public transport connections and the distribution of everyday destinations (Geurs & van Wee, 2004; Bruno et al., 2024).

For Burnie, the findings suggest that planning responses should consider both the availability of transport alternatives and the spatial distribution of essential services.

Nevertheless, the analysis cannot determine whether individual accessibility gaps are primarily caused by service frequency, waiting times, transfers, distance or other factors. These would require further investigation before specific interventions could be recommended.

## 5. Limitations

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings.

**SA1-level representation:** Accessibility was calculated using representative origins for each SA1. These estimates describe area-level accessibility and may not reflect the circumstances of every household.

**Travel-time threshold:** The analysis uses a 30-minute threshold to measure reachable destinations. This provides a consistent basis for comparison but does not capture differences in individual travel-time preferences or needs.

**Public transport conditions:** Bus accessibility depends on the modelled transport network, timetables and walking connections. Actual journeys may differ because of service reliability and individual travel requirements.

**Equal category weighting:** The six service categories contributed equally to the overall accessibility score. This does not account for differences in service importance, quality or residents' individual needs.

**Socio-economic data:** The CDI–IRSD comparison includes 54 SA1s with available data. IRSD represents relative area-level disadvantage and cannot establish the socio-economic circumstances of individual residents (ABS, 2023).

**Relative accessibility:** CDI measures the accessibility difference between private cars and public buses. A high CDI does not necessarily indicate good accessibility by either mode.

These limitations are important because accessibility is influenced by the interaction of transport systems, land use and individual circumstances (Geurs & van Wee, 2004).

## 6. Overall Interpretation

The analysis demonstrates that private cars provide a substantial accessibility advantage over public buses across much of Burnie.

However, the findings also show that **car dependency and overall accessibility are not equivalent**. Some areas exhibit high relative car dependency despite having limited access to essential services.

The positive relationship between CDI and IRSD further indicates that car dependency does not consistently overlap with greater socio-economic disadvantage.

Together, these findings highlight the importance of examining **private-car accessibility, public transport accessibility and socio-economic conditions together** when investigating spatial inequalities.

This integrated approach provides a basis for identifying areas that may benefit from further accessibility assessment and supports broader discussions about equitable transport planning in regional cities.
